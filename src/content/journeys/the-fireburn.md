## The Fireburn of 1878

On October 1, 1878, thirty years after emancipation, the laborers of St. Croix rose up against the oppressive conditions that had replaced slavery in name only. The **Labor Act of 1849** had bound formerly enslaved people to year-long contracts on the very plantations where they had been enslaved, leaving them trapped in a cycle of poverty and exploitation.

The revolt that erupted became known as **"The Fireburn"** — because the laborers set fire to the plantations, the sugar fields, and the town of Frederiksted itself. Over the course of several days, more than fifty estates were burned.

## The Queens

At the heart of this uprising were three extraordinary women, known to history as the **Queens of the Fireburn**:

### Queen Mary Thomas

Mary Thomas was the most prominent leader. She rallied the laborers and led the march on Frederiksted. Colonial authorities later arrested her and sentenced her to death, though her sentence was eventually commuted to imprisonment in Denmark. She became, and remains, a symbol of resistance and the fight for justice.

### Queen Agnes

Agnes worked alongside Mary, organizing workers and directing the revolt across the plantations. Her leadership was instrumental in the scale of the uprising.

### Queen Mathilda

Mathilda, too, played a central role, ensuring the revolt spread across the island. Together, the three Queens embodied the fury and the hope of an entire people who refused to accept subjugation.

## Legacy

The Fireburn forced the Danish colonial government to finally reform the oppressive labor laws. It remains the most significant act of resistance in the post-emancipation history of the Danish West Indies, and the three Queens are honored as national heroes in the U.S. Virgin Islands today.

In 2018, a statue of Queen Mary was unveiled in Copenhagen, Denmark — a powerful acknowledgment of the colonial past and the people who fought against it.
