# Camille Pissarro: The St. Thomas Years

When art historians discuss the origins of French Impressionism, they typically focus on the cafes of Paris or the misty landscapes of the French countryside. However, one of the founding fathers of the entire movement—and the only artist to exhibit his work in all eight of the historic Impressionist exhibitions—was born and raised in the bustling, tropical heat of the Danish West Indies. 

His name was Camille Pissarro, and the story of his artistic genius begins in the harbor of St. Thomas.

## Born in Charlotte Amalie

He was born **Jacob Abraham Camille Pizarro** on July 10, 1830, in Charlotte Amalie. He was the son of a prominent French-Jewish merchant, Frederick Pizarro, and a local Creole woman, Rachel Petit. 

Growing up in the Jewish community of St. Thomas, young Camille was expected to eventually take over his father's lucrative mercantile business. He was sent to boarding school in France for a formal education, where he developed an early appreciation for European art masters, but he was summoned back to St. Thomas at age 17 to work as a clerk in his father’s shop on Dronningens Gade (Main Street).

## The Rebellious Clerk

Camille despised the life of a merchant. Instead of balancing ledgers and managing cargo manifests, he spent every spare moment sketching. 

St. Thomas in the mid-19th century was an incredibly dynamic subject for a young artist. It was a thriving, chaotic free port. Camille would wander down to the wharves to sketch the massive sailing ships from around the world. He was particularly drawn to the everyday lives of the working class. He filled his notebooks with sketches of Black market women carrying baskets of produce on their heads, laborers hauling cargo, and donkeys traversing the steep "step streets" of Charlotte Amalie.

## Meeting Fritz Melbye and the Escape

Pissarro's life changed dramatically in 1852 when he met Fritz Melbye, a young, established Danish painter who had stopped in St. Thomas while traveling through the Americas. 

Melbye immediately recognized Camille's raw talent and encouraged him to pursue art full-time, rather than languishing as a merchant's clerk. In an act of rebellion against his family's expectations, the 22-year-old Pissarro packed his bags and essentially ran away with Melbye to Caracas, Venezuela. They spent two years there, painting intensely and honing their skills. 

In 1855, Pissarro finally moved to Paris, leaving the Caribbean behind permanently. He officially changed his name from the Portuguese "Pizarro" to the French "Pissarro" and threw himself into the Parisian art scene, eventually befriending Claude Monet, Paul Cézanne, and Edgar Degas, and helping to launch the Impressionist revolution.

## The Caribbean Influence on Impressionism

Although Pissarro lived the rest of his life in Europe, art historians note that his formative years in the Danish West Indies deeply influenced his revolutionary artistic style. 

The intense, vibrant sunlight of the Caribbean helped shape his later fascination with capturing natural light and atmosphere—a cornerstone of Impressionism. Furthermore, unlike many of his European contemporaries who focused on painting wealthy aristocrats or mythical scenes, Pissarro had a lifelong dedication to painting ordinary, working-class people (often peasants working in the fields). This egalitarian focus was almost certainly rooted in his early days in St. Thomas, where he preferred sketching the enslaved and free Black laborers in the market rather than the wealthy colonial elites. 

Today, Pissarro is celebrated globally as a master of French art, but the foundation of his vision was undeniably forged in the Danish West Indies.
