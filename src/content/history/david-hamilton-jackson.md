# David Hamilton Jackson: The Fight for a Free Press

In the twilight years of the Danish West Indies, the majority Black population was technically free from slavery, but they remained economically and politically oppressed. The planters controlled the wages, and the colonial government controlled the flow of information. 

It was within this stifling environment that one of the Virgin Islands' most revered heroes emerged: an educator, labor leader, and journalist named **[David Hamilton Jackson](/history/david-hamilton-jackson)**.

## The Government Monopoly on News

By the early 20th century, the Danish colonial government maintained a strict, state-sponsored monopoly on the press. The only newspaper allowed to operate was heavily censored, ensuring that the laboring class had no public platform to voice their grievances, organize, or expose the harsh realities of their working conditions on the sugar estates of St. Croix. 

Without a free press, the planters could maintain their economic stranglehold, keeping wages abysmally low and suppressing any attempts at unionization.

## The 1915 Mission to Denmark

[David Hamilton Jackson](/history/david-hamilton-jackson), a charismatic and highly educated young man from St. Croix, recognized that true labor reform was impossible without a voice. 

In 1915, Jackson took an incredibly bold step. Funded by small donations collected from the working class across St. Croix, he boarded a ship and traveled all the way to Copenhagen. His mission was to bypass the hostile local colonial government and petition the King of Denmark, Christian X, directly. 

In Denmark, Jackson was remarkably successful. He gave fiery speeches, met with Danish politicians, and secured an audience with the King. He eloquently explained the plight of the Crucian laborers and successfully convinced the Crown to lift the oppressive censorship laws, granting the Danish West Indies a free press.

## *The Herald* and the Labor Movement

Jackson returned to St. Croix in late 1915 to a hero's welcome. Utilizing his hard-won victory, he immediately established the first independent newspaper in the islands, **_The Herald_**. 

_The Herald_ became a powerful weapon. Jackson used the printing press to relentlessly expose the abuses of the planter class, educate the laborers on their rights, and advocate for massive economic reform.

> "A change is coming, a change must come, for the people will no longer be silent... We demand a living wage, we demand decent treatment, we demand that our humanity be recognized."
> — David Hamilton Jackson, Editorial in *The Herald*, 1915

Empowered by the ability to communicate openly, Jackson then tackled his next major goal: organized labor. He founded the **St. Croix Labor Union**, the very first labor union in the territory's history. Under his leadership, the union quickly grew to include thousands of agricultural workers. 

In 1916, Jackson organized a massive, island-wide strike on St. Croix. The workers refused to harvest the sugarcane until their demands were met. For a month, the island's economy stood still. Ultimately, the planters capitulated, agreeing to significantly raise wages and improve working conditions.

## A Lasting Legacy: Liberty Day

Jackson continued to be a fierce advocate for civil rights after the United States purchased the islands in 1917, fighting against the segregationist policies of the US Naval Administration alongside figures like Rothschild Francis. 

Today, [David Hamilton Jackson](/history/david-hamilton-jackson) is rightfully celebrated as a monumental figure in Virgin Islands history. Every year on November 1st—the anniversary of the day he printed the very first issue of _The Herald_—the territory celebrates **Liberty Day** (also widely known as D. Hamilton Jackson Day). It is a public holiday honoring the man who gave the people a voice and proved the undeniable power of a free press and organized labor.
