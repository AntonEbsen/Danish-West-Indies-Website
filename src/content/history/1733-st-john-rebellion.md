# The 1733 St. John Rebellion

The 1733 slave rebellion on the island of St. John is one of the earliest, most sustained, and most significant uprisings of enslaved people in the history of the Americas. For six months, the colonial economy of the island was entirely halted as African rebels held control against the Danish authorities.

## The Catalyst: The Akwamu and the 1733 Slave Codes

By 1733, the enslaved population on St. John heavily outnumbered the European colonists. A significant portion of this population consisted of the **Akwamu**, an ethnic group from the Gold Coast (present-day Ghana). 

Many of the enslaved Akwamu were not born into slavery; they were former nobles, warriors, and merchants who had been captured during conflicts in West Africa and sold to the Danish West India and Guinea Company. They brought with them significant military experience and organizational skills.

The immediate catalyst for the rebellion was a combination of severe drought, devastating hurricanes, and an insect plague that destroyed the enslaved people's provision grounds, leading to mass starvation. In response to growing unrest, the colonial administration passed the brutal **1733 Slave Codes**, which mandated amputations, brandings, and hangings for even minor offenses.

## The Seizure of Fortsberg

The rebellion was meticulously planned. On the morning of November 23, 1733, a group of enslaved men arrived at Fortsberg, the Danish fort in Coral Bay, carrying bundles of wood. Hidden inside the wood were cane bills (machetes). 

Upon gaining entry, they quickly overpowered the Danish garrison, killed the soldiers, and fired the fort's cannon. This served as the signal for enslaved people across the island's plantations to rise up. They systematically attacked the estates, burning the sugar cane fields and forcing the surviving European planters to flee the island via small boats to nearby St. Thomas.

## Six Months of Control

For the next six months, the rebels, led by figures such as King June and Kanta, held the island. The Danish colonial forces on St. Thomas launched multiple expeditions to retake St. John, but they were repeatedly repelled by the well-organized Akwamu guerrilla tactics. 

The rebellion caused an absolute cessation of economic output for the Danish West India and Guinea Company on the island, creating a financial crisis for the colonial administration.

## The Suppression

Denmark, lacking the military strength to suppress the rebellion on its own, was forced to beg for assistance from rival colonial powers. 

In April 1734, a heavy contingent of French troops arrived from the neighboring island of Martinique. The professional French military, combined with local militias and enslaved people promised freedom for fighting against the rebels, slowly forced the Akwamu into the rugged interior of the island. 

Facing capture and the horrific tortures prescribed by the Danish slave codes, many of the rebel leaders chose to end their own lives rather than return to bondage. 

## The Aftermath

The 1733 rebellion profoundly shook the Danish colonial enterprise. It demonstrated the fragile hold the Europeans had over their massive enslaved populations and forced planters to militarize their estates even further. It stands today as a powerful testament to the enduring resistance against chattel slavery in the Caribbean.
