# The Mechanics of the 1917 Transfer: Gold, Submarines, and the Dannebrog

The story of how the Danish West Indies became the United States Virgin Islands is often summarized as a simple real estate transaction. However, the actual mechanics of the sale in 1917 were driven by global warfare, diplomatic anxiety, and a staggering sum of physical gold. 

It was the culmination of over 50 years of on-and-off negotiations between Washington and Copenhagen.

## The Catalyst: German Submarines

By 1915, Europe was consumed by the horrors of World War I. While the United States was technically still neutral, American military strategists were increasingly panicked about the security of the newly constructed Panama Canal. 

The primary fear was the formidable German Imperial Navy and its fleet of U-boats (submarines). If Germany were to conquer Denmark (which remained neutral but highly vulnerable), they could easily claim the Danish West Indies as a spoil of war. The deep-water harbor of Charlotte Amalie on St. Thomas would be a perfect, impenetrable Caribbean base for German U-boats to strike American shipping and threaten the canal. 

Driven by this existential military anxiety, US Secretary of State Robert Lansing approached the Danish government with a blunt offer: sell the islands to the United States now, or the US might be forced to seize them by military force to prevent them from falling into German hands.

## The Price: $25 Million in Gold

Facing intense diplomatic pressure and realizing that the colonies were an economic drain on the national treasury, Denmark agreed to negotiate. 

The price was set at an astronomical **$25 million** (equivalent to roughly $600 million today). Unlike the Louisiana Purchase or the purchase of Alaska, which were paid via bank drafts and bonds, this transaction was uniquely tangible. The United States paid Denmark in actual, physical gold coin. 

This made the Virgin Islands the most expensive land purchase in US history per square acre. For comparison, the US bought the massive territory of Alaska for just $7.2 million.

## The 1916 Plebiscite

Before the treaty could be finalized, it had to be approved by the Danish public. In December 1916, Denmark held a national plebiscite (referendum) on the sale. 

The Danish populace, weary of subsidizing a distant, unprofitable colony and fearful of being dragged into a conflict with the United States, voted overwhelmingly in favor of the sale (approximately 283,000 in favor to 158,000 against). 

Notably, the actual residents of the Danish West Indies—the people whose nationality and future were being sold—were completely excluded from this vote. They had no formal say in the transfer of their own homeland.

## March 31, 1917: Lowering the Dannebrog

The official transfer took place on **March 31, 1917**. Somber ceremonies were held simultaneously in Charlotte Amalie, Christiansted, and Frederiksted. 

On St. Thomas, the Danish Governor Henri Konow and the US Navy Commander Edwin T. Pollock stood before a military honor guard. As a military band played the Danish national anthem, the *Dannebrog*—the flag that had flown over the islands for nearly 250 years—was slowly lowered. Many Danish officials and residents reportedly wept openly. 

Moments later, the American *Stars and Stripes* was raised to the tune of "The Star-Spangled Banner," accompanied by a booming 21-gun salute from warships in the harbor. 

A week later, on April 6, 1917, the United States officially declared war on Germany and entered World War I, proving that the strategic military urgency behind the $25 million purchase was very real.
