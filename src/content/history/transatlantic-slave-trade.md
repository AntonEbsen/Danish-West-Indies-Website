# The Transatlantic Slave Trade

For nearly 150 years, Denmark was an active and highly profitable participant in the transatlantic slave trade. Under the Danish flag, an estimated 100,000 to 120,000 enslaved Africans were forcibly transported across the Atlantic to labor on the sugar, cotton, and indigo plantations of the Danish West Indies (St. Croix, St. Thomas, and St. John).

## The Triangle Trade

Denmark's involvement in the slave trade was managed primarily through the **Danish West India and Guinea Company** (Vestindisk-Guineisk Kompagni) until the Danish Crown took direct control in 1754. The trade operated on a ruthless, highly organized triangular route:

1. **Outward Bound (Denmark to West Africa):** Ships departed from Copenhagen or Flensburg loaded with firearms, gunpowder, textiles, alcohol, and manufactured goods. These were traded at Danish forts on the Gold Coast (modern-day Ghana), such as Fort Christiansborg in Accra, in exchange for captive Africans.
2. **The Middle Passage (West Africa to the Caribbean):** The captives were crammed into the unventilated holds of slave ships in horrific conditions. The journey took several months, and disease, malnutrition, and despair led to staggering mortality rates—often exceeding 15-20% per voyage.
3. **The Return Voyage (Caribbean to Denmark):** The surviving captives were sold at auction in Charlotte Amalie (St. Thomas) or Christiansted (St. Croix). The ships were then loaded with raw sugar, molasses, and rum produced by enslaved labor, and sailed back to Copenhagen, where the raw goods were refined and sold at an enormous profit across Europe.

## Fort Christiansborg and the Gold Coast

The Danish presence in West Africa was centered around **Fort Christiansborg** (Osu Castle), established in the 1660s. Here, Danish factors (merchants) negotiated with local African elites and kingdoms—such as the Akwamu and later the Ashanti—who controlled the inland supply of captives (often prisoners of war or kidnapping victims).

The captives were held in dungeons beneath the fort for months until a Danish ship arrived to transport them across the Atlantic. 

## The Abolition of the Trade (1792 / 1803)

In the late 18th century, influenced by Enlightenment ideals, economic recalculations, and mounting international pressure (particularly from British abolitionists), Denmark became the first European nation to formally decree the end of its transatlantic slave trade. 

On March 16, 1792, King Christian VII (via Finance Minister Ernst Schimmelmann) issued a decree to abolish the trade. However, the decree included a **10-year transition period**.

### The Dark Decade (1792-1802)
Rather than immediately ending the suffering, the 10-year grace period led to a massive, horrific spike in human trafficking. Danish planters were encouraged to purchase as many enslaved people (specifically women of childbearing age) as possible before the ban took effect in 1803. The goal was to make the enslaved population on the islands self-sustaining through natural reproduction.

During this final decade, Danish ships transported tens of thousands of Africans in some of the most concentrated slaving voyages in the nation's history.

## Slavery Continued

It is critical to distinguish between the abolition of the *trade* and the abolition of *slavery itself*. When the transatlantic slave trade officially became illegal under Danish law on January 1, 1803, the institution of chattel slavery on the plantations of St. Croix, St. Thomas, and St. John continued unabated for another 45 years. 

Generations of enslaved people continued to suffer and die in the cane fields until they seized their own freedom during the Emancipation Rebellion of 1848.
