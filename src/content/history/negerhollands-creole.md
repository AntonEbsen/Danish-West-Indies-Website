# Negerhollands: The Extinct Creole of the Danish West Indies

It is one of the great historical ironies of the Danish West Indies that for the vast majority of its 250-year existence as a Scandinavian colony, almost no one actually spoke Danish. 

The Danish administration and the military spoke Danish, but they were a tiny minority. The islands were a chaotic linguistic melting pot of enslaved Africans speaking various West African dialects, Dutch planters, English smugglers, French privateers, and Sephardic Jewish merchants. Out of this necessity for communication, a completely new, unique language was born: **Negerhollands**.

## The Birth of a Creole

Negerhollands (literally translating to "Negro-Dutch") emerged in the late 17th and early 18th centuries, primarily on the islands of St. Thomas and St. John. 

Despite Denmark owning the islands, the earliest and most prominent European settlers on St. Thomas were actually Dutch colonists fleeing other Caribbean islands. When enslaved Africans were brought to the islands, they had to quickly develop a way to communicate not only with their European enslavers but also with other enslaved people who spoke entirely different African languages. 

They took the vocabulary of the Dutch planters, mixed it with words from Danish, English, and Spanish, and structured it using the grammar and syntax of West African languages (like Akan and Ewe). The result was Negerhollands—a rich, highly expressive Creole language.

## The *Lingua Franca* of the Islands

By the mid-18th century, Negerhollands wasn't just the language of the enslaved population; it had become the absolute *lingua franca* of the Danish West Indies. 

If a Danish planter wanted to run his estate, he had to learn Negerhollands. White Creole children born on the islands often learned Negerhollands from their enslaved nurses before they ever learned to speak European languages. 

The language gained significant formal recognition when the Moravian missionaries arrived in the 1730s. Realizing that preaching in standard German or Dutch was useless, the missionaries mastered Negerhollands. They even codified the language, producing the first Negerhollands dictionaries and grammar books, and remarkably, they translated the entire New Testament into Negerhollands so the enslaved population could read it.

## The Shift to English

Negerhollands remained the dominant language on St. Thomas and St. John for over a century. However, the linguistic tide began to shift in the 19th century.

St. Croix, which was purchased later (1733), had been heavily settled by English planters, and an English-based Creole began to dominate there. Furthermore, as St. Thomas exploded into a massive international free port, English became the language of commerce. By the time of the 1848 emancipation, English was rapidly overtaking Negerhollands as the primary language of the working class. When the Danish government finally instituted compulsory public education in the mid-19th century, the instruction was surprisingly given in English, not Danish, further sealing the fate of the old Creole.

## Extinction

By the time the United States purchased the islands in 1917, Negerhollands was already severely endangered, spoken only by the oldest generations in the rural, isolated parts of St. Thomas and St. John. The American era, with its strict English-only education policies, accelerated the decline.

The language is now classified as officially extinct. The last known native speaker of Negerhollands, a woman named Alice Stevens from St. John, passed away in 1987. Today, the language lives on only in the Moravian archives, a fascinating linguistic fossil of the complex, diverse, and brutal history of the Danish West Indies.
