# Queen Mary Thomas: The Face of the Fireburn

When the laborers of St. Croix rose up in the **1878 [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix)**, they were not a disorganized mob. They were led by a group of formidable women who would come to be known in oral history as the "Queens of the [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix)." Among them—Axeline "Queen Agnes" Elizabeth Salomon, Mathilda "Queen Mathilda" McBean, and Susanna "Bottom Belly" Abramsen—one figure stands out as the iconic face of the resistance: **[Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas)**.

Her story is not just one of a riot; it is a case study in the harsh economic realities of post-emancipation labor and the lengths to which the Danish state went to crush those who challenged its financial order.

## The Economic Context of Resistance

To understand [Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas), one must understand the economic trap of the 1849 Labor Act. Though slavery was abolished in 1848, the Labor Act bound laborers to plantations through one-year contracts that could only be renegotiated on a single day each year: October 1st, known as "Contract Day."

Wages were fixed at poverty levels (typically 10 to 15 cents a day), and laborers were frequently paid in goods or "allowances" rather than cash, tying them to plantation stores. When October 1, 1878 arrived, rumor spread that the promised improvements to wages and conditions were not going to materialize. The frustration boiled over into the [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix)—a massive, coordinated destruction of the economic infrastructure that was oppressing them: the sugar estates.

## Leadership and The [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix)

[Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas), then in her early 30s and a worker at Estate Sprat Hall, emerged as a fiery and charismatic leader during the riot. Armed with a cane bill (machete) and matches, she was seen directing crowds, challenging plantation managers, and demanding better wages.

The rebellion was highly targeted. The rioters burned over 50 plantations and half the town of Frederiksted, specifically attacking the economic engines of the Danish planters while generally sparing human lives (though some planters and many laborers were killed in the ensuing violence and military crackdown). [Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas)'s leadership was so absolute that workers referred to her as "Queen Mary."

## Trial and Imprisonment in Copenhagen

The Danish military violently suppressed the [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix), and the retaliation was swift and brutal. Hundreds of laborers were arrested, and many were summarily executed. [Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas) and the other "Queens" were captured and subjected to a military tribunal.

[Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas) was sentenced to death for her role in the rebellion. However, the Danish Crown, perhaps wishing to avoid creating martyrs or bowing to international pressure regarding their colonial practices, commuted her sentence to life imprisonment with hard labor.

In 1882, [Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas), along with Queen Agnes and Queen Mathilda, was transported across the Atlantic to Denmark. They were incarcerated in the **Kvindefængslet på Christianshavn** (The Women's Prison in Christianshavn), Copenhagen. For a Black Caribbean woman to be imprisoned in the cold, foreign environment of 19th-century Copenhagen was a profound isolation, yet it underscores how seriously the Danish state took the economic threat they posed.

## Return and Legacy

[Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas) spent five years in the Danish prison before being sent back to Christiansted, St. Croix, in 1887 to serve out the remainder of her sentence. She eventually died in 1905, but her legacy only grew.

Today, Queen Mary is a folk hero in the U.S. Virgin Islands, immortalized in songs, statues, and oral tradition. In 2018, a striking monument titled **"I Am Queen Mary"** was unveiled in Copenhagen, created by artists Jeannette Ehlers and La Vaughn Belle. Placed in front of the West India Warehouse, the monument is a powerful visual reclamation of history, forcing Denmark to confront the face of the woman who challenged its colonial economic machine.
