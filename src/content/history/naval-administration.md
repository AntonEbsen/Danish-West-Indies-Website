# Rule by the Navy: The Early American Era

When the Dannebrog was lowered and the Stars and Stripes were raised over St. Thomas, St. John, and St. Croix on March 31, 1917, the residents of the newly minted "United States Virgin Islands" anticipated a new era of democracy and American prosperity. However, what they received was not immediate full citizenship, but rather 14 years of strict military rule.

The United States had not purchased the islands for their economic potential; they purchased them for military strategy. Fearing that Germany might seize the Danish West Indies to establish a submarine base during World War I, the US paid $25 million to secure the deep-water harbor of Charlotte Amalie. Consequently, the islands were placed under the direct control of the United States Navy.

## The Naval Administration (1917–1931)

For the first 14 years of American rule, the highest authority in the Virgin Islands was not an elected official or even a civilian governor, but an Admiral of the US Navy. 

The Naval Administration brought significant structural improvements to the islands. The Navy built a modern hospital, improved sanitation, paved roads, and constructed water catchment systems to fight the constant threat of drought. The mortality rate plummeted under naval medical care.

However, these infrastructural gains came at a steep social cost. The islands were treated less like a territory of citizens and more like a massive naval vessel. Strict military discipline was imposed on the civilian population. The naval governors wielded near-autocratic power, commanding the police force, overseeing the judiciary, and heavily influencing the colonial councils (which were still elected by a tiny fraction of the wealthiest landowners, a holdover from Danish rule).

## Clashes of Culture and the Introduction of Jim Crow

The transition was jarring. Virgin Islanders, particularly the Black majority, were accustomed to a complex, nuanced social hierarchy under Danish rule that was based more on class and property than strict racial segregation. 

The arrival of the US Navy abruptly introduced rigid American "Jim Crow" racial attitudes. Many naval officers and enlisted men, hailing from the segregated American South, were unaccustomed to interacting with a confident, educated Black middle class. Friction was immediate and constant. 

Virgin Islanders found themselves suddenly subjected to racial slurs, segregated seating in public spaces, and blatant discrimination in hiring for government and naval positions. Furthermore, the naval administration heavily censored the local press, shutting down newspapers that dared to criticize naval policies.

## Rothschild Francis and the Fight for Rights

The oppressive atmosphere of the Naval Administration catalyzed a fierce local civil rights movement. The most prominent leader of this movement was **Rothschild Francis**, a charismatic shoemaker, journalist, and labor leader from St. Thomas.

Francis founded a newspaper called *The Emancipator* to openly attack the abuses of the Naval Administration. He argued passionately that Virgin Islanders, living under the American flag, were entitled to the protections of the US Constitution and full American citizenship. 

The Navy despised Francis. He was repeatedly harassed, arrested on dubious charges of criminal libel, and imprisoned. Despite this persecution, Francis traveled to Washington D.C. to lobby Congress directly, exposing the autocratic nature of naval rule in the Virgin Islands to the American public and the ACLU.

## The Transition to Civilian Rule

The relentless pressure from local leaders like Rothschild Francis, combined with growing embarrassment in Washington over the Navy's heavy-handed tactics, eventually forced a change. 

In 1927, Congress finally granted US citizenship to the residents of the Virgin Islands. Four years later, in 1931, President Herbert Hoover officially ended the Naval Administration, transferring control of the islands to the Department of the Interior and appointing the first civilian governor, Paul M. Pearson. 

While the end of naval rule was a victory for local activists, the struggle for full self-determination and equal rights in the US Virgin Islands was far from over.
