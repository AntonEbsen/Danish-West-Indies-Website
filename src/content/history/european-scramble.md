# The Battle for the Caribbean: Early European Settlements

As the 17th century dawned, the myth of Spanish invincibility in the Americas was crumbling. Recognizing that Spain was overextended and unable to protect its vast territorial claims, rival European powers—primarily the English, Dutch, and French—rushed into the Caribbean to carve out their own sugar colonies. 

The Virgin Islands, strategically located and largely unoccupied by the Spanish, became a chaotic chessboard where these nations violently vied for control before the Danes finally consolidated their rule.

## The Divided Island: St. Croix

The struggle for dominance was most intense on St. Croix, the largest of the Virgin Islands. During the 1620s and 1630s, the island experienced a bizarre and tense shared occupation.

English and Dutch settlers arrived on St. Croix almost simultaneously, establishing separate, rival settlements. For a few years, an uneasy truce existed, but the close quarters inevitably led to conflict. In 1645, a series of assassinations between the English and Dutch governors sparked an open war on the island. The English ultimately prevailed, violently expelling the Dutch settlers. 

However, the English victory was short-lived. In 1650, a Spanish expeditionary force from Puerto Rico, fearful of a permanent Protestant stronghold so close to their borders, launched a surprise attack and massacred or expelled the remaining English.

## The French and the Knights of Malta

The Spanish occupation of St. Croix lasted only a few months. Sensing an opportunity, a French fleet arrived, ousted the Spanish, and claimed the island for the Kingdom of France. 

In a fascinating historical twist, the French Crown soon transferred the administration of St. Croix to the **Knights of Malta**, a wealthy Catholic military order. The Knights ruled the island from 1651 to 1665. They attempted to build plantations and fortifications, but the venture was a massive financial failure marred by disease and mismanagement. The Knights eventually sold the island back to the French West India Company.

Under French rule, St. Croix continued to struggle. By 1695, facing threats from the English, the French King ordered the complete evacuation of St. Croix, relocating all settlers and enslaved people to Saint-Domingue (modern-day Haiti). For decades, St. Croix was left abandoned to nature.

## The English in the North

While St. Croix changed hands rapidly, the English focused their efforts on the northern Virgin Islands (which would eventually become the British Virgin Islands, such as Tortola and Virgin Gorda). However, they also laid frequent, overlapping claims to St. Thomas and St. John, leading to constant diplomatic friction.

## Paving the Way for Denmark

The constant warfare, piracy, and economic failures of the English, Dutch, and French in the Virgin Islands created a unique opportunity. 

Denmark, a smaller European power eager to join the lucrative transatlantic sugar and slave trades, realized that the abandoned or loosely held Virgin Islands were ripe for the taking. In 1672, the Danish West India and Guinea Company successfully established a permanent settlement on St. Thomas. They followed by claiming St. John in 1718, and finally, they purchased the abandoned island of St. Croix from the French in 1733.

The chaotic European scramble had ended, and the era of the Danish West Indies had officially begun.
