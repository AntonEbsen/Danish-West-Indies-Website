# Peter von Scholten: The Complex Emancipator

On July 3, 1848, standing on the ramparts of Fort Frederik before a massive uprising of enslaved laborers, Governor-General Peter von Scholten uttered the words that ended chattel slavery in the Danish West Indies: *"Now you are free, you who are here, and all on the islands."*

Because of this decree, Danish history has frequently painted von Scholten as a humanitarian hero and the benevolent savior of the enslaved. However, a rigorous examination of the colonial ledgers and historical context reveals a deeply complex, contradictory figure who was fundamentally a product—and an administrator—of a brutal system.

## The Colonial Administrator

Peter von Scholten spent most of his adult life in the Danish West Indies, rising through the military and administrative ranks to become Governor-General in 1827. For over two decades, he was the chief executive of a colony whose entire economic existence relied on the torture and forced labor of enslaved Africans.

Von Scholten was a man of the empire. He enforced the harsh colonial laws that kept the sugar economy churning, and he personally owned enslaved individuals who worked in his household. He amassed significant wealth and power by maintaining the status quo for the Danish Crown and the wealthy planters of St. Croix.

## The Reformer

Despite his position, von Scholten did enact several controversial reforms aimed at "ameliorating" the conditions of slavery, likely in an attempt to stave off the outright rebellions that were erupting in neighboring British colonies.

Under his administration, the colonial government established schools for enslaved children (though instruction was highly controlled and heavily influenced by the Moravian church). He also sought to integrate the "Free People of Color" (Frikulørte) into the colonial militia and civil service, attempting to create a buffer class between the white European elite and the massive enslaved population.

His most profound personal connection to the colonized population was his long-term partnership with **Anna Heegaard**, a wealthy and prominent Free Woman of Color. They lived together openly as a couple at his estate, Bülowsminde, scandalizing the European planter class. There is little doubt that Heegaard's perspective significantly influenced his policies.

## The Emancipation Decree

When the massive rebellion led by General Buddhoe erupted in Frederiksted in 1848, von Scholten was faced with an impossible choice. The Danish military garrison was vastly outnumbered. He knew that attempting to suppress the rebellion by force would result in a horrific bloodbath and the total destruction of the island's sugar infrastructure.

His declaration of emancipation was less a proactive humanitarian gesture and more a desperate, pragmatic calculation to save the colony from burning to the ground. 

## The Fall

The reaction from the European planter class and the Danish government was immediate and furious. They accused von Scholten of cowardice and treason for capitulating to the rebellion. 

Broken by the stress of the uprising and the vitriol of the planters, von Scholten suffered a severe nervous breakdown within days of the decree. He was forced to resign in disgrace and quietly slipped out of St. Croix under the cover of night.

Upon his return to Copenhagen, he was court-martialed for treason and dereliction of duty. Although he was eventually acquitted of the most severe charges by the Danish Supreme Court, he died a broken and marginalized man in Altona (then part of Denmark) in 1854.

## Legacy

Peter von Scholten's legacy cannot be simplified into a neat binary of hero or villain. He was the man who spoke the words of freedom, but those words were forced from his lips by the organized resistance of the enslaved people themselves. He is a testament to the inescapable contradictions of those who attempt to wield absolute power within an inherently unjust system.
