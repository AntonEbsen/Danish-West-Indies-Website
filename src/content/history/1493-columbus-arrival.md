# 1493: Columbus and the "Eleven Thousand Virgins"

While indigenous peoples had inhabited the islands for thousands of years, the archipelago's introduction to the European world—and the name it bears today—originated in the late 15th century. In 1493, during his second voyage to the Americas, Christopher Columbus sighted the sprawling, intricate chain of islands and cays.

## The Second Voyage (1493)

Columbus’s second expedition was a massive undertaking compared to his first. He set sail from Spain in September 1493 with a fleet of 17 ships and around 1,200 men, aiming to establish permanent Spanish colonies, expand the Spanish empire, and spread Christianity. 

In November 1493, after making landfall on islands further south like Dominica and Guadeloupe, the fleet sailed northward. As they navigated through the Caribbean Sea, they encountered a dense archipelago of mountainous islands, small cays, and hidden coves that seemed to stretch endlessly across the horizon.

## Naming the "Virgin Islands"

Awed by the sheer number of islands clustered together, Columbus was reminded of a popular medieval European legend: the story of Saint Ursula. 

According to Catholic tradition, Saint Ursula was a Romano-British princess who, along with 11,000 virginal handmaidens, embarked on a pan-European pilgrimage before being martyred by the Huns in Cologne. Looking at the myriad of small landmasses emerging from the sea, Columbus named the archipelago ***Santa Úrsula y las Once Mil Vírgenes*** (Saint Ursula and the 11,000 Virgins). 

Over time, this long and unwieldy name was shortened simply to *Las Vírgenes*, or "The Virgin Islands."

## First Encounters and the Salt River Incident

Columbus did not merely sail past the islands. His fleet made a brief, fateful stop at an island the indigenous Taino called *Ay Ay*, which Columbus renamed *Santa Cruz* (now known as St. Croix).

Anchoring near what is today the **Salt River Bay**, Columbus sent a small landing party ashore in a longboat to search for fresh water and capture indigenous people. The Spanish encountered a small canoe carrying Carib (Kalinago) men and women. A skirmish erupted—often cited as the first recorded armed conflict between Europeans and indigenous peoples in the Americas. 

During the fight, arrows were exchanged for musket fire. Several indigenous people were killed or captured, and at least one Spaniard was mortally wounded by a poisoned arrow. 

## The Aftermath of Discovery

Columbus and his fleet did not stay long, continuing their journey toward Hispaniola and Puerto Rico. However, this brief encounter marked the beginning of a devastating new era. The Spanish laid claim to the islands but largely ignored them for colonization, finding them too small and lacking the vast gold deposits they sought elsewhere.

Nevertheless, the islands were now on European maps. This "discovery" paved the way for subsequent waves of European explorers, pirates, and eventually the intense colonial rivalries between the English, Dutch, French, and Danes that would define the Virgin Islands for centuries to come.
