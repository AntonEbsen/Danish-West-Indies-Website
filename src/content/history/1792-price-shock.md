# The 1792 Price Shock: Market Warping and Human Trafficking

When King Christian VII issued the 1792 Royal Decree to abolish the transatlantic slave trade, it was hailed as a humanitarian victory. However, a closer look at the economic mechanics of the decree reveals a much darker reality. 

Because the decree included a **10-year transition period** (delaying the actual ban until 1803), it inadvertently created one of the most extreme, artificial market shocks in the history of the colonial Caribbean.

## The Economics of Anticipated Scarcity

The fundamental law of supply and demand dictates that if a market anticipates a total cessation of supply in the near future, the immediate demand—and the resulting price—will skyrocket. 

When the Danish planters realized that their supply of captive labor from West Africa would be permanently cut off in exactly ten years, panic set in. The mortality rate on the sugar estates was so high that without a constant influx of new laborers, the plantations would inevitably collapse. The planters determined they had to hoard as much "human capital" as possible before the 1803 deadline.

## State-Subsidized Trafficking

The Danish government, led by Finance Minister (and major plantation owner) Ernst Schimmelmann, actively manipulated the market to ensure the planters could afford this massive purchasing spree. The state engineered a series of economic policies specifically designed to accelerate human trafficking during the "Dark Decade" (1792-1802):

1.  **State Loans:** The Danish government provided highly favorable, state-backed loans to planters on St. Croix, St. Thomas, and St. John, exclusively for the purpose of buying newly imported Africans.
2.  **Tax Exemptions:** To lower the barrier to entry, the government abolished the standard import tariffs on enslaved people arriving from Africa.
3.  **Export Bans:** To trap the labor supply within the Danish economy, it was made strictly illegal to sell or export enslaved people from the Danish West Indies to neighboring foreign islands.

## The Shift in Demographic Value

The most insidious economic effect of the 1792 price shock was the sudden, massive shift in the demographic value of human beings. 

Prior to 1792, the highest market prices were generally paid for young, strong men who could perform the most brutal physical labor in the sugar fields. However, the impending ban on the transatlantic trade forced a paradigm shift: the colonial economy had to become self-sustaining through natural reproduction.

Suddenly, the biological reproductive capacity of enslaved women became the most valuable commodity in the colony. 
*   The Danish state completely eliminated the head tax on enslaved women.
*   The market prices for young, captive women skyrocketed, as planters realized that purchasing a woman was the only way to secure a future generation of free, enslaved labor for their estates.

## The Tragic Result

The economic manipulation of the 1792 decree resulted in a catastrophic acceleration of the slave trade. Driven by state loans and the panic of impending scarcity, Danish slave ships crammed tens of thousands of Africans across the Middle Passage in the final decade of the 18th century. 

Rather than easing the suffering of the enslaved, the economic mechanics of the 10-year grace period proved to be one of the most concentrated and heavily subsidized periods of human trafficking in Danish history.
