# Pricing the Enslaved: The Matrikler Data

To understand the economics of the Danish West Indies, one must confront a chilling historical reality: the total financial commodification of human life. The Danish colonial administration was meticulous in its bookkeeping. Every year, colonial officials compiled the **matrikler** (tax ledgers), which recorded the property of every plantation owner on St. Croix, St. Thomas, and St. John. 

In these ledgers, enslaved Africans were inventoried alongside horses, cattle, and copper boiling vats. By analyzing this data, economic historians can decode exactly how the colonial market priced human beings.

## The Variables of Value

The price of an enslaved person (measured in Danish *Rigsdaler*) was not fixed; it fluctuated based on a cold calculus of perceived economic utility. The primary variables dictating market value were:

1.  **Age and Physical Capability:** The highest prices were commanded by "Prime" field hands—typically young men and women between the ages of 16 and 25, who were deemed capable of surviving the most grueling physical labor in the cane fields.
2.  **Specialized Skills:** Enslaved people who possessed highly specialized skills—such as carpenters, masons, coopers (barrel makers), and master sugar boilers—were valued significantly higher than standard field laborers. A master sugar boiler, whose expertise could dictate the quality and profit margin of an entire estate's harvest, was considered an incredibly valuable asset.
3.  **Gender:** While men often commanded slightly higher prices for heavy field labor, women held a unique dual-value for the planters: their physical labor in the fields, and their biological reproductive capacity (which became the central focus of the colonial economy after 1792).
4.  **Origin:** Colonial ledgers often distinguished between "Bozales" (newly arrived captives from Africa) and "Creoles" (enslaved people born in the Caribbean). Creole slaves were often priced higher, as they were already acclimated to the diseases of the environment and spoke the local Creole languages, making them "ready" for immediate exploitation without the high mortality risk of the "seasoning" process.

## Human Depreciation

Perhaps the most brutal economic reality revealed by the *matrikler* is the concept of depreciation. Modern businesses depreciate the value of heavy machinery as it wears down over time. The Danish planters applied this exact same accounting principle to human beings.

As an enslaved person aged, or as their body broke down under the horrific strain of 16-hour workdays and malnutrition, their taxable value in the ledgers was steadily reduced. Elderly enslaved people (those who managed to survive past the age of 40 in the cane fields), the blind, and the severely injured were often listed in the ledgers as "Manqueret" (defective or useless) and were assessed at zero financial value. 

In the eyes of the Danish colonial economy, a human being only possessed value as long as their body could generate profit. 

## The Economic Archive

Today, the *matrikler* provide one of the most comprehensive datasets available for studying the economics of Caribbean slavery. While they are documents built entirely on oppression, they are now vital tools for historians and economists working to understand the scale of the wealth extracted from the Danish West Indies, and the exact mechanisms of the system that extracted it.
