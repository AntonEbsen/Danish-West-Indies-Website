# Profit Margins of the Middle Passage

Behind the horrific human suffering of the transatlantic slave trade lay a sophisticated, highly calculated financial machine. For Danish merchants operating out of Copenhagen and Flensburg in the 18th century, a slaving voyage to the Gold Coast and the Caribbean was not just a trade route; it was a high-risk, high-reward investment vehicle.

To understand how the Middle Passage operated for so long, one must analyze the brutal profit margins that sustained it.

## Financing a Voyage

Equipping a slave ship was an incredibly expensive endeavor. A merchant had to purchase the ship, pay the crew for a voyage that could last up to two years, and acquire the trade goods (firearms, textiles, alcohol) needed to purchase captives in West Africa.

To mitigate this massive upfront cost and distribute the financial risk, Danish merchants pioneered early forms of joint-stock investing. The cost of a voyage was divided into "shares." Wealthy aristocrats, politicians, and even everyday middle-class citizens in Copenhagen could buy a fraction of a slaving voyage. If the ship returned successfully laden with sugar, the profits were paid out as dividends to the shareholders. This system meant that the wealth generated by human trafficking was deeply embedded across all levels of the Danish economy.

## The Calculus of "Cargo Loss"

The most chilling aspect of the Middle Passage's financial mechanics was how merchants calculated the horrific mortality rates of the enslaved.

Danish captains had to make a ruthless economic decision regarding the packing of the ships:
*   **"Tight Packing":** Cramming as many human beings into the unventilated holds as physically possible. This resulted in staggering mortality rates (often 15% to 25% from disease and suffocation), but the sheer volume of captives meant that even if a quarter of the people died, the remaining survivors would still yield a massive profit in the Caribbean.
*   **"Loose Packing":** Taking fewer captives to reduce the mortality rate, ensuring that a higher percentage of the "cargo" arrived alive and healthy to fetch prime prices at auction in Christiansted.

To protect their investments, merchants purchased insurance on the voyages. However, insurers treated enslaved human beings strictly as property. If captives died from disease (the most common cause of death), it was often considered a standard risk of the trade and was not paid out. However, if captives were thrown overboard to save a sinking ship, or died during an uprising, the insurance companies would reimburse the merchants for the "lost cargo."

## The Ultimate Return on Investment (ROI)

The true profit of the triangle trade was not generated by selling enslaved people in the West Indies. The captives were often sold at a break-even price, or even at a slight loss.

The astronomical profits were generated on the **third leg** of the triangle. The money made from auctioning the captives was immediately used to purchase raw cane sugar and rum from the St. Croix plantations. 

Because enslaved labor kept the production cost of this sugar artificially close to zero, the ships returned to Copenhagen with holds full of incredibly cheap "White Gold." When this sugar was refined and sold across the European market, the returns on investment for a single successful voyage could exceed 200% to 300%. 

It was this final, massive profit margin that blinded the Danish state to the atrocities of the trade, driving the colonial engine for over a century.
