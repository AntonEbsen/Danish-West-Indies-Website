# The Moravian Missionaries: The Religion of Compliance

When examining the mechanisms of colonial control in the Danish West Indies, it is essential to look beyond the military garrisons and the brutal slave codes. One of the most effective instruments for maintaining the colonial hierarchy was not the whip, but the pulpit.

In 1732, the Danish Crown permitted the Moravian Brethren (the *Herrnhuters*), a Protestant denomination from Germany, to establish missions on St. Thomas. They soon expanded to St. Croix and St. John, establishing prominent estates like New Herrnhut, Friedensthal, and Friedensfeld. Their presence profoundly shaped the culture and the economics of the islands.

## The Trade-Off: Education for Submission

The Moravian missionaries are often credited in traditional histories with bringing education and healthcare to the enslaved population. Unlike the Danish Lutheran church, which largely ignored the enslaved and catered only to the European elite, the Moravians actively lived among and preached to the African population. 

They were instrumental in developing "Dutch Creole," the lingua franca of the islands, and even translated the New Testament into this language to teach enslaved people to read.

However, this education came with a massive ideological caveat. The colonial administration allowed the Moravians access to the plantations because the missionaries promised the planters a more docile workforce. The core of the Moravian theology presented to the enslaved population was the **religion of compliance**. 

The missionaries preached that slavery was a divinely ordained lot on earth. They taught that true spiritual salvation could only be achieved through strict obedience to earthly masters, hard work, and the total rejection of "heathen" African spiritual practices (such as the Bamboula dance). To rebel against the planter was framed as a rebellion against God.

## The Paradox of the Mission Estate

The most glaring contradiction of the Moravian presence in the Danish West Indies was their participation in the very economic system they were theoretically sent to spiritually reform.

The Moravian missions were not merely churches; they were functioning, profitable agricultural estates. To sustain their missionary work and generate wealth for the church back in Europe, the Moravian Brethren purchased and owned enslaved human beings. 

The enslaved people owned by the church worked the mission's sugar cane fields and maintained the missionary compounds under conditions that were virtually indistinguishable from the secular plantations surrounding them. The missionaries found theological justifications for this, arguing that by keeping enslaved people within the physical boundaries of the mission, they were protecting their souls from the corrupting influence of the broader colonial society.

## Medicine and Power

As historian Niklas Thode Jensen has extensively documented, the Moravians also intertwined theology with medical care. The missionaries frequently acted as physicians for the enslaved population. However, this healthcare was rarely an act of pure humanitarianism.

Like the plantation owners, the Moravians viewed the physical health of the enslaved population through an economic lens—a healthy enslaved person could work longer hours in the cane fields. Furthermore, they used medical care as a proselytizing tool, offering treatment to those who converted and attended church services, thereby using bodily vulnerability as leverage for spiritual control.

## A Complex Legacy

The legacy of the Moravian missionaries in the Danish West Indies is fraught with paradox. They undoubtedly provided the only formal education available to the enslaved population for over a century, fostering literacy and a deeply rooted Afro-Caribbean Christian culture that endures in the Virgin Islands today.

Yet, this was achieved through a deliberate strategy of social control. The Moravians acted as spiritual enforcers for the colonial state, leveraging theology to suppress resistance and justify the brutal economics of the sugar trade—a trade in which they were active, complicit participants.
