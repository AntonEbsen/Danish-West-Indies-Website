# Osu Castle & The Danish Gold Coast

To fully understand the colonial ledgers of the Danish West Indies, one must look across the Atlantic to the origin point of the data: the Danish forts on the Gold Coast (in present-day Ghana). 

Denmark's Caribbean empire was not a bilateral relationship; it was a triangular trade built entirely on the extraction of human beings from West Africa. The epicenter of this extraction was **Christiansborg Castle**, locally known as **Osu Castle**, located in Accra.

## The Danish Foothold in Africa

Beginning in the late 17th century, the Danish West India and Guinea Company established a series of forts and trading posts along the Gold Coast. Christiansborg Castle served as the headquarters of the Danish colonial administration in Africa.

These forts were not sprawling colonies; they were heavily fortified commercial outposts. The Europeans rarely ventured inland because they lacked the military capacity and were highly susceptible to tropical diseases like malaria (the mortality rate for Danish soldiers and merchants stationed at Osu was astronomically high).

Instead, the Danes relied entirely on economic alliances and military treaties with powerful local African states.

## The Economics of Extraction

The transatlantic slave trade was driven by local geopolitics as much as European demand. The Danish merchants formed strategic alliances with powerful inland kingdoms, most notably the **Akwamu** and later the **Asante** empire.

These African kingdoms engaged in expansionist wars against their neighbors. The captives taken in these conflicts were marched to the coast and sold to the Danish merchants at Osu Castle. 

The colonial ledgers document the brutal economic exchange that took place within the walls of the castle. Human beings were traded for European manufactured goods shipped from Copenhagen, including:
* Firearms and gunpowder (which fueled further inland wars and captive-taking)
* Textiles (Indian cottons and European linens)
* Brass basins and copper bars
* **Cruzan Rum** (produced by enslaved labor in the Danish West Indies)

## The Dungeons of Christiansborg

Once purchased, enslaved people were branded with the mark of the Danish West India and Guinea Company. They were then imprisoned in the dark, suffocating dungeons beneath Christiansborg Castle. 

They were held in these horrific conditions for weeks or months until a Danish slave ship arrived from Copenhagen. From the dungeons, they were marched through the "Door of No Return," loaded onto small boats, and rowed out through the heavy surf to the waiting ships.

## The Middle Passage to St. Croix

The journey from the Gold Coast to the Danish West Indies—the Middle Passage—was a nightmare of disease, starvation, and violence. The ledgers kept by the ship captains were meticulous, recording the exact number of people embarked at Osu Castle and the number of "losses" (deaths) during the voyage.

It was these individuals, surviving the dungeons of Osu and the horrors of the Atlantic crossing, who were then unloaded in Christiansted or Charlotte Amalie and entered into the colonial ledgers of the Danish West Indies as property. 

## The End of the Danish Gold Coast

Following Denmark's legal abolition of the transatlantic slave trade in 1803, the economic viability of the Gold Coast forts plummeted. The Danes attempted to establish plantations in Africa to replace the slave trade, but these ventures failed miserably. 

In 1850, seeing no further profit, Denmark sold Christiansborg Castle and all its remaining possessions on the Gold Coast to the British Empire for £10,000. Today, Osu Castle remains a profound monument to the violent origins of the Danish colonial economy.
