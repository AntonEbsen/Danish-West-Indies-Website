# The 1792 Decree: The Abolition of the Transatlantic Slave Trade

On March 16, 1792, King Christian VII of Denmark issued a Royal Decree that formally prohibited the transportation of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic under the Danish flag. This decree made Denmark the first European colonial power to legally ban the transatlantic slave trade, predating the British and American bans by 15 years. 

For many years, this was taught in Danish schools as a triumph of Enlightenment humanitarianism. However, modern historical research paints a far more cynical and economically driven picture.

> "With the beginning of the year 1803, all trade in Negroes from our subjects' side on the coast of Africa and elsewhere shall cease... During the intervening time, from now until the end of the year 1802, it shall be permitted to all nations without exception, and under all flags, to import Negroes to Our West Indian Islands."
> — Royal Decree of Christian VII, March 16, 1792

## The Great Slave Trade Commission (1792)

In the late 18th century, the horrific realities of the Middle Passage were becoming widely known in Europe. British abolitionists like Thomas Clarkson were gaining immense political traction, and it was becoming clear to European economists that the slave trade might soon face international prohibition.

In Denmark, Finance Minister **Ernst Schimmelmann** (himself the owner of multiple St. Croix sugar plantations) spearheaded the "Great Slave Trade Commission." The commission was tasked with determining whether the Danish West Indies could survive economically without the continuous importation of captive Africans.

### The Economic Recalculation
The commission concluded that the transatlantic slave trade was, ironically, becoming a financial liability for the Danish state. 
*   The mortality rates on the slave ships were so high that voyages frequently lost money.
*   The death rates on the plantations in the Danish West Indies were so catastrophic that the planters were locked in a perpetual, expensive cycle of buying new enslaved people to replace those who were worked to death.

The commission determined that it would be more profitable in the long run to make the enslaved population on the islands **self-sustaining through natural reproduction**, rather than relying on constant, expensive importation from Africa.

## The "Dark Decade" (1792 - 1802)

While the decree to ban the trade was issued in 1792, it included a massive, deliberate loophole: a **10-year transition period**. The ban would not take effect until January 1, 1803.

## The Distinction: Trade vs. Slavery

It is a critical historical distinction that the 1792 decree **did not abolish slavery**. It only abolished the *transportation* of new captives from Africa. 

When the ban finally took effect in 1803, the enslaved men, women, and children already trapped on St. Croix, St. Thomas, and St. John remained legally classified as property. They, and any children they subsequently bore, were forced to endure another 45 years of brutal chattel slavery. True abolition of the institution would not occur until the enslaved population violently demanded it during the **1848 Emancipation Rebellion**.

## Legacy

Denmark's status as the "first" to abolish the slave trade is historically accurate in a legal sense, but morally compromised in practice. The 1792 decree was not an act of pure humanitarianism; it was an economic pivot engineered by plantation owners like Ernst Schimmelmann to protect their long-term profits while extracting one final, massive wave of human capital from West Africa.
