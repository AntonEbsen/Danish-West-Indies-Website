# The Economic Power of the Market Women

When examining the history of the Danish West Indies, the focus is often placed on the white Danish planters who exported sugar or the merchants who traded in the free port of St. Thomas. However, the internal, day-to-day economy of the islands—the actual flow of food and essential goods—was dominated by an incredibly resilient and powerful demographic: the Black market women.

Both during slavery and long after emancipation, these women were the undisputed economic engines of the colonial towns. 

## The Sunday Markets

During the era of slavery, Danish planters typically did not provide enough food to sustain their enslaved workforce. Instead, they allowed the enslaved population to cultivate small "provision grounds" on the rocky, less fertile edges of the plantations. 

On Sundays—the only day of rest—enslaved women would walk miles from the country estates into the towns of Christiansted, Frederiksted, and Charlotte Amalie. There, they established vibrant, bustling markets. They sold the surplus provisions they had grown (yams, cassava, bananas), alongside charcoal they had burned, and fish that had been caught. 

These Sunday markets became the literal lifelines of the islands. Even the white Danish population and the garrisoned soldiers came to rely entirely on the Black market women for their daily sustenance. 

## Purchasing Freedom

The market was one of the very few avenues where an enslaved person could accumulate hard currency. 

The women who ran the market stalls were shrewd negotiators and sharp businesswomen. Over years of grueling labor and careful saving, many of these women achieved the ultimate goal: they accumulated enough Danish Rigsdalers to purchase their own freedom, or the freedom of their children, from their enslavers. 

Because of this economic engine, free women of color became a distinct, growing, and influential property-owning class in the Danish West Indies long before the official 1848 emancipation.

## The Information Network

The market squares were not just economic hubs; they were the absolute center of communication in the Danish West Indies. 

Women from every plantation across the island would converge in one place, exchanging news, rumors, and vital information. If a slave ship had arrived, if a harsh new law was being passed in Copenhagen, or if a rebellion was brewing on a neighboring island, the market women knew about it first. They were the primary news broadcasters and the connective tissue that linked the isolated rural estates together.

## The Backbone of Resistance: The "Queens"

This combination of economic independence, sharp communication networks, and social influence made the market women formidable figures. It is no coincidence that when organized resistance occurred, women were frequently at the forefront. 

The most famous example is the 1878 labor revolt on St. Croix, known as the **[Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix)**. The leaders of this massive rebellion were not men, but a group of formidable women—[Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas), Axeline Elizabeth Salomon, Mathilda McBean, and Susanna Abramsen—who are immortalized today as the "Queens of the Virgin Islands." While [Mary Thomas](/history/mary-thomas) ("Queen Mary") is the most well-known, they all possessed deep respect and organizational influence within the laboring communities, an influence heavily rooted in the matriarchal power structures built around the island markets.

## A Lasting Legacy

Long after the decline of the sugar industry, the market women remained a fixture of Virgin Islands society. They negotiated prices, controlled local supply chains, and served as the financial anchors for their families. Their legacy is a profound reminder that even within the most oppressive economic systems, marginalized people can carve out spaces of immense power and independence.
