# The 1867 Disasters: Hurricane, Earthquake, and Tsunami

Life in the Caribbean has always been defined by a deep, cautious respect for the forces of nature. However, the late autumn of 1867 subjected the Danish West Indies to a triad of apocalyptic natural disasters so severe that they not only destroyed towns and livelihoods but actually altered the geopolitical trajectory of the islands.

Within a span of less than a month, the islands were battered by a massive hurricane, shattered by a severe earthquake, and inundated by a towering tsunami.

## The San Narciso Hurricane (October 29, 1867)

The terror began on October 29th with the San Narciso Hurricane. Striking late in the season, the Category 3 storm caught the islands completely off guard. 

The bustling harbor of Charlotte Amalie in St. Thomas, typically a safe haven, became a trap. Over 80 ships were sunk, dismasted, or driven ashore by the fierce winds and storm surge. Hundreds of sailors and local residents drowned. On land, the destruction was nearly absolute—roofs were torn from buildings, wooden homes were splintered, and the sugar estates of St. Croix were flattened, destroying the year's crop.

## The Earthquake (November 18, 1867)

As the stunned population was still clearing the debris and burying the dead, the earth violently gave way. 

On the afternoon of November 18, 1867, a massive 7.3 magnitude earthquake struck the Anegada Trough, located between the Virgin Islands and St. Croix. The tremors were so violent that stone buildings which had miraculously survived the hurricane collapsed into dust. Chimneys fell, and the ground reportedly rolled in visible waves.

## The Tsunami

The earthquake was only a precursor to the sea’s wrath. Shortly after the shaking subsided, the ocean rapidly retreated, leaving fish flopping on the exposed harbor floors. Minutes later, a massive tsunami crashed into the islands. 

In St. Thomas, a wall of water estimated to be 20 feet high surged into the harbor, tossing ships into the town's streets. On St. Croix, the devastation was equally surreal. The *USS Monongahela*, an American warship anchored at Frederiksted, was lifted by the tsunami and carried over the warehouses, before being deposited high and dry into the town's streets. Miraculously, few on the ship died, but the visual of a massive naval vessel sitting in the middle of a Caribbean town became the defining image of the disaster.

## Geopolitical Fallout: Seward’s Folly Averted?

The timing of these disasters had massive political implications. At the exact moment the islands were being torn apart, US Secretary of State William H. Seward was actively finalizing a treaty with Denmark to purchase St. Thomas and St. John for $7.5 million.

The United States Navy desperately wanted the deep-water harbor of Charlotte Amalie for a Caribbean naval base. However, when news of the hurricane, earthquake, and tsunami reached Washington D.C., American public opinion turned sharply against the purchase. Critics in Congress mocked the idea of buying a "ruined, hurricane-swept, earthquake-shaken" territory. 

The US Senate ultimately refused to ratify the treaty. The Danish West Indies would remain under the Dannebrog (the Danish flag) for another 50 years until the successful transfer in 1917.

## Resilience and Rebuilding

The death toll from the triad of 1867 disasters was staggering, and the economic damage accelerated the financial decline of the Danish West Indies. Yet, the disasters also highlighted the incredible resilience of the islanders. Left largely to fend for themselves with minimal aid from Copenhagen, the local population of newly emancipated laborers, merchants, and artisans slowly cleared the rubble, righted the ships, and rebuilt their towns stone by stone.
