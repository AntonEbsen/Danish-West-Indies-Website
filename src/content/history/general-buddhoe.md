# General Buddhoe and the Bloodless Rebellion of 1848

When discussing the 1848 abolition of slavery in the Danish West Indies, history books often focus heavily on Governor-General [Peter von Scholten](/history/peter-von-scholten), the Danish official who issued the fateful decree. However, von Scholten did not grant freedom out of sudden benevolence; his hand was forced by a massive, brilliantly organized, and remarkably disciplined uprising led by an enslaved man named **John Gottlieb**, forever known in Virgin Islands history as **[General Buddhoe](/history/general-buddhoe)**.

## The Brewing Storm

By the summer of 1848, the enslaved population of St. Croix was at a breaking point. Slavery had already been abolished in the neighboring British Virgin Islands (1834) and the French islands (earlier in 1848). 

The Danish King had promised a gradual emancipation, decreeing in 1847 that children born to enslaved mothers would be free, but adults would have to wait another 12 years. This decree satisfied no one. The enslaved population on St. Croix decided they would not wait over a decade for a freedom that was already theirs by right. 

## The Architect of the Revolt: [General Buddhoe](/history/general-buddhoe)

John Gottlieb ([General Buddhoe](/history/general-buddhoe)) was an enslaved man on the La Grange estate near Frederiksted. He was a skilled artisan—a sugar boiler—which gave him a degree of mobility and respect among both the enslaved population and the planters. 

Buddhoe possessed exceptional leadership skills. In secret meetings across the western end of St. Croix, he organized a massive, coordinated strike. His most incredible achievement was insisting on absolute discipline: the rebellion was to be completely non-violent. He understood that if white blood was spilled, the Danish military would respond with overwhelming, indiscriminate slaughter, just as they had during the 1733 St. John rebellion.

## July 3, 1848: The March on Frederiksted

On the morning of July 3, 1848, the plan was executed flawlessly. Thousands of enslaved people across the western estates laid down their tools, blew conch shells to signal the uprising, and marched on the town of Frederiksted. 

An estimated 8,000 people converged on the fort. True to Buddhoe's strict orders, they were armed with sticks and agricultural tools, but they did not attack the white citizens or the soldiers. They simply surrounded Fort Frederik and issued a stark ultimatum: they wanted their freedom immediately, or they would burn the town to the ground.

Buddhoe acted as the primary negotiator, keeping the massive, furious crowd under control while Danish officials panicked inside the fort.

## The Decree and the Tragic Aftermath

The local authorities sent a frantic message across the island to Christiansted to fetch Governor [Peter von Scholten](/history/peter-von-scholten). Arriving in Frederiksted that afternoon, von Scholten looked at the overwhelming, disciplined crowd and realized the era of slavery was over. Without waiting for authorization from Copenhagen, he declared: *"All unfree in the Danish West Indies are from today emancipated."*

The rebellion was a total success, achieved without a single casualty on July 3rd. 

Tragically, [General Buddhoe](/history/general-buddhoe) did not get to enjoy the freedom he had won for his people. In the paranoid aftermath of the rebellion, conservative Danish planters and military officers (furious at von Scholten's decree) arrested Buddhoe. Fearing that executing him would spark a violent riot, they instead exiled him. Buddhoe was placed on a ship bound for Trinidad, and he completely disappears from the historical record after that voyage. 

While his ultimate fate remains a mystery, [General Buddhoe](/history/general-buddhoe) is forever celebrated in the Virgin Islands as the hero who orchestrated the bloodless revolution.
