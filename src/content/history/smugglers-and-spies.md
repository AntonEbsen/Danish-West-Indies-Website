# Smugglers and Spies: St. Thomas During the Global Wars

For much of the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the Atlantic world was engulfed in relentless warfare. From the American Revolution to the sweeping devastation of the Napoleonic Wars, European powers blockaded each other's ports and seized each other's merchant ships. 

However, the Kingdom of Denmark maintained a strict policy of neutrality. This diplomatic stance transformed the Danish West Indies—specifically the deep, sheltered harbor of Charlotte Amalie on St. Thomas—into one of the wealthiest, most vital, and most chaotic ports in the Americas.

## The American Revolution: A Lifeline for Rebels

When the thirteen American colonies rebelled against Great Britain in 1775, the British Navy immediately attempted to blockade the American coast to starve the rebels of weapons and supplies. 

St. Thomas, as a neutral Danish free port, quickly became a critical lifeline for George Washington’s Continental Army. American blockade runners would sail south to Charlotte Amalie, where they could openly purchase French gunpowder, Dutch muskets, and Danish provisions without fear of British interception while in port. 

The harbor was a hotbed of espionage. British spies monitored the docks, noting which American ships were loading munitions, while American agents brokered covert deals in the taverns. The Danish governors, prioritizing profit, happily turned a blind eye to the blatant smuggling, reaping massive customs revenues in the process.

## The Napoleonic Boom

The economic boom reached its zenith during the Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815). As France and Britain waged total war, neither nation's merchant ships were safe on the open ocean. 

To bypass the dangers, merchants from across Europe and the Americas routed their goods through St. Thomas. The harbor of Charlotte Amalie was frequently packed with hundreds of ships at a time. The town’s population exploded with an influx of foreign merchants, fleeing French aristocrats from Saint-Domingue (Haiti), privateers, and opportunists. 

It was during this period that the town's iconic merchant warehouses—with heavy iron doors facing the street and private wharves extending directly into the harbor—were constructed to handle the massive volume of transiting cargo. St. Thomas became the "Emporium of the West Indies."

## A Safe Haven for Privateers

Because St. Thomas was neutral territory, it offered a rare sanctuary where enemies were forced to dock side-by-side. 

A French privateer who had just captured an English merchant vessel could sail his prize into Charlotte Amalie. Under the protection of the Danish fort's cannons, the privateer could legally auction off the stolen English goods and refit his ship. English merchants, in turn, were known to attend these auctions in St. Thomas to buy back their own stolen cargo at a discount. It was a bizarre, highly lucrative ecosystem built entirely on the loopholes of international maritime law.

## The British Occupations

This immense profitability and blatant circumvention of British blockades eventually drew the ire of London. 

Suspecting that Danish neutrality was heavily favoring the French, the British Empire briefly dropped its respect for the Dannebrog. In 1801, and again in 1807, massive British naval fleets arrived in the Virgin Islands and forced the Danish governors to surrender. 

During the second occupation, which lasted until 1815, the British completely blockaded the islands, temporarily halting the massive flow of illicit trade. When the islands were finally returned to Denmark following the fall of Napoleon, St. Thomas resumed its role as a regional trading hub, but the wild, wildly profitable "Golden Age" of smugglers and spies had reached its end.
