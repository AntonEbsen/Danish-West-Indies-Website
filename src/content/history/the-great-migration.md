# The Great Migration: The Harlem Connection

When the Danish flag was lowered in 1917 and the Danish West Indies became the United States Virgin Islands, the transition was not accompanied by sudden economic prosperity. In fact, the early decades of American rule were characterized by profound economic depression.

The collapse of the sugar industry, which had been the singular focus of the colonial economy for centuries, left thousands of Virgin Islanders without viable means of supporting their families. The response to this economic crisis was a massive demographic shift: **The Great Migration**.

## The Collapse of the Sugar Economy

The economic engine of St. Croix had been failing long before the Americans arrived. The abolition of slavery in 1848, the breaking of the exploitative labor contracts in the 1878 [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix), and the devastating competition from European sugar beets had rendered Caribbean cane sugar largely unprofitable.

When the U.S. Navy took administrative control of the islands in 1917, they offered little in the way of economic development or agricultural diversification. The passage of the Volstead Act (Prohibition) in the United States in 1920 delivered a final, devastating blow, legally shutting down the St. Croix rum industry, which had been one of the few remaining sources of export revenue.

Facing poverty and starvation wages, Virgin Islanders looked northward.

## The Journey to Harlem

In the 1920s and 1930s, thousands of Virgin Islanders packed onto steamships heading for New York City. By some estimates, nearly a quarter of the islands' population migrated during this period.

Because they were now under U.S. jurisdiction (though they were not granted full U.S. citizenship until 1927), immigration was theoretically easier than for other Caribbean nationals. The vast majority settled in **Harlem**, joining the massive influx of African Americans moving north from the Jim Crow South, as well as immigrants from other Caribbean islands like Jamaica and Barbados.

## The Economy of Remittances

The migration to Harlem was fundamentally an economic survival strategy for families back in the Virgin Islands. 

Virgin Islanders in New York worked in factories, domestic service, maritime trades, and the burgeoning service industry. Despite facing intense racism and segregation in the United States, they were able to earn significantly higher wages than were possible on St. Croix or St. Thomas.

A massive portion of these wages was packaged up and mailed directly back to family members in the Virgin Islands. These **remittances** became a vital pillar of the struggling island economy. The money sent from Harlem kept families fed, allowed them to repair hurricane-damaged homes, and maintained the social fabric of the islands during the darkest years of the Navy administration.

## The Harlem Renaissance and Political Awakening

The Virgin Islanders who migrated did not just send money; they became central figures in the cultural and political explosion known as the **Harlem Renaissance**.

Virgin Islanders were known for their high literacy rates and fierce political organizing. Figures like **Hubert Harrison** (the "father of Harlem radicalism") and **Casper Holstein** (a wealthy numbers runner who became a major philanthropist and civil rights advocate) emerged as leading voices.

They used their platform in New York to fiercely advocate for civil rights, not just in America, but for their families back home. They lobbied Washington D.C. relentlessly, protesting the authoritarian rule of the U.S. Navy and eventually helping to secure U.S. citizenship for Virgin Islanders in 1927, and civilian government in 1931.

## A Transnational Legacy

The Great Migration established a permanent, transnational pipeline between the Virgin Islands and New York. To this day, the economic and cultural ties between the islands and the mainland diaspora remain incredibly strong, a legacy born from the ashes of the colonial sugar economy.
