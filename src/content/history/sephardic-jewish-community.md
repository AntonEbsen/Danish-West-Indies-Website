# A Haven for the Diaspora: The Sephardic Jewish Community of St. Thomas

Amidst the chaos of colonial rivalries, piracy, and the brutality of the slave trade, the island of St. Thomas emerged as a rare sanctuary for religious freedom in the Caribbean. Because of a unique combination of Danish law and economic pragmatism, Charlotte Amalie became a thriving hub for Sephardic Jews, deeply shaping the cultural and commercial history of the Virgin Islands.

## Fleeing Persecution

The Sephardic Jewish diaspora has a long, painful history of forced migration. Following the Alhambra Decree of 1492, which expelled Jews from Spain, and the subsequent Portuguese Inquisition, many Sephardic families fled to Amsterdam. From the Netherlands, they migrated to the Caribbean, establishing communities in Dutch colonies like Brazil, Curaçao, and St. Eustatius.

However, safety in the Caribbean was often precarious. In 1781, during the American Revolution, the British Navy ruthlessly sacked the Dutch island of St. Eustatius. The British targeted the wealthy Jewish merchants, seizing their property and violently expelling the community. 

## The Appeal of Danish Tolerance

Looking for a new refuge, many of the families expelled from St. Eustatius—alongside Jews fleeing unrest in other colonies—looked to St. Thomas. 

The Danish West Indies offered two massive incentives. First, King Christian V of Denmark had decreed in the 17th century that St. Thomas would be open to settlers of all religious backgrounds, a rare policy of tolerance in an era dominated by state religions. Second, St. Thomas was a neutral "free port," making it the most lucrative trading hub in the Americas. 

Jewish merchants, utilizing their extensive family networks across Europe and the Americas, quickly integrated into the economy of Charlotte Amalie. They became ship owners, auctioneers, and powerful wholesale merchants, handling the immense flow of goods passing through the neutral Danish port during the Napoleonic Wars.

## The Historic St. Thomas Synagogue

As the community grew in wealth and size, they sought to build a permanent house of worship. In 1833, they constructed the **Synagogue of the Congregation Beracha Veshalom Vegmiluth Hasidim** (Blessing and Peace and Loving Deeds). 

Today, it is the second-oldest synagogue in continuous use in the Western Hemisphere, and the oldest under the American flag. The building itself is an architectural marvel, featuring thick stone walls (built from local rubble and yellow Flensborg bricks), mahogany pews, and beautiful Baccarat crystal chandeliers.

The most famous feature of the St. Thomas Synagogue, however, is its floor, which is entirely covered in clean, white sand. While the exact origin of this tradition is debated, the most common explanation is that it serves as a powerful reminder of the Spanish Inquisition, when Sephardic Jews had to practice their faith in secret, using sand on the floors of their cellars to muffle the sound of their prayers.

## A Lasting Legacy

The Jewish community of St. Thomas produced several figures of global historical significance. The most famous was **Camille Pissarro**, one of the founding fathers of French Impressionism, who was born into a prominent French-Jewish mercantile family in Charlotte Amalie in 1830. Another notable figure was Judah P. Benjamin, born in St. Croix to Jewish parents, who later became a highly controversial but prominent politician in the United States and the Confederacy.

While the population of the Jewish community declined in the late 19th century as the commercial importance of St. Thomas waned, the legacy of this Sephardic diaspora remains firmly anchored in the historic sand-floored synagogue that still stands on Crystal Gade today.
