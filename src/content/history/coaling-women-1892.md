# The Coaling Women and the 1892 Strike

In the latter half of the 19th century, the global maritime industry underwent a massive technological shift: the transition from wind-powered sailing ships to coal-powered steamships. 

While the sugar economy of the Danish West Indies was in terminal decline, the deep, protected harbor of Charlotte Amalie on St. Thomas found a highly lucrative new purpose. It became one of the most important coaling stations in the Caribbean, servicing massive trans-Atlantic vessels, most notably those of the German Hamburg-America Line. But the engine that actually powered this new economy was not a machine—it was the backs of Black women.

## The Grueling Labor of the Coaling Women

Unlike modern ports with automated cranes and conveyor belts, the coaling of steamships in St. Thomas in the late 1800s was done entirely by manual labor. 

When a steamship arrived, hundreds of laborers would line up on the docks. The overwhelming majority of these workers were Black women. Their job was unimaginably grueling: they had to balance heavy wicker baskets on their heads, each filled with up to 100 pounds of dirty, dusty coal, and carry them up steep wooden gangplanks to dump into the ship's bunkers. 

They did this barefoot, singing rhythmic work songs to maintain a steady pace. They were paid by the basket, receiving a small token for each load dumped, which they could cash in at the end of the day.

## The Mexican Silver Crisis

The catalyst for unrest did not stem from the brutal nature of the work itself, but from how the women were compensated. 

During this era, the Danish West Indies had a chaotic currency system. The shipping companies paid the coaling women in Mexican silver dollars, which were a common regional trade currency. However, in the early 1890s, the global value of silver plummeted. 

The coaling women realized that the Mexican silver they were being paid with was depreciating rapidly. When they tried to use their hard-earned silver in the local shops of Charlotte Amalie, merchants were refusing to accept it at face value, essentially slashing the women's real wages while the cost of living remained the same.

## The 1892 Strike and Queen Coziah

Frustration reached a boiling point in September 1892. Recognizing their immense leverage—steamships were sitting idle in the harbor, losing money every hour they couldn't load coal—the coaling women organized a massive strike.

The leader of this strike was a formidable woman named **Queen Coziah** (born Coziah Ndongo). Much like Queen Mary during the [Fireburn](/history/1878-fireburn-st-croix) on St. Croix 14 years earlier, Queen Coziah commanded deep respect among the working class. 

On September 12, 1892, Queen Coziah led hundreds of coaling women marching through the streets of Charlotte Amalie. They armed themselves with sticks and stones, shutting down the harbor and bringing the entire economy of St. Thomas to a grinding halt. They demanded that the shipping companies stop paying them in worthless Mexican silver and start paying them in stable, valuable Danish gold or US dollars.

## A Decisive Victory

The Danish authorities and the wealthy shipping agents were panicked by the sheer scale and organization of the women's strike. They quickly realized that they could not break the strike without risking widespread violence and catastrophic economic losses.

Within days, the shipping companies capitulated. They agreed to the women's demands, confirming that all future wages for coaling would be paid in Danish gold coin. 

The 1892 Coaling Strike stands as a monumental, though often overlooked, victory for labor rights in the Virgin Islands. It proved the incredible economic power of working-class Black women and cemented Queen Coziah's legacy as a hero of the labor movement.
