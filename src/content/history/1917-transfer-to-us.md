# The 1917 Transfer: Selling the Danish West Indies

On March 31, 1917, over two and a half centuries of Danish colonial rule in the Caribbean came to an abrupt end. The Danish flag (the Dannebrog) was lowered, the Stars and Stripes were raised, and the Danish West Indies officially became the **United States Virgin Islands**.

This transfer was the result of a massive transaction: the United States purchased the islands from Denmark for **$25 million in gold**. But why did Denmark finally sell the colonies that had generated so much of its national wealth?

## The Economic Decline

By the dawn of the 20th century, the Danish West Indies had shifted from an economic crown jewel to a financial burden for the Danish state.

The golden age of Caribbean sugar had collapsed. As explored in our article on the *Sugar Beet Threat*, the rise of government-subsidized European sugar beets decimated the global price of cane sugar. Without the brutal efficiency of unpaid enslaved labor (which had ended in 1848), and without the restrictive contracts of the Labor Act (broken by the 1878 Fireburn), the St. Croix sugar estates could no longer compete on the global market. 

Simultaneously, St. Thomas, which had thrived as a free port and a crucial coaling station for steamships, saw its economic relevance plummet. The advent of modern shipping and the opening of the Panama Canal in 1914 shifted global trade routes, bypassing the Danish islands. 

The Danish parliament found itself subsidizing the colonial administration year after year, turning domestic public opinion against maintaining the empire.

## The Geopolitics of World War I

While Denmark wanted to sell for economic reasons, the United States wanted to buy for purely military ones.

The U.S. had previously attempted to purchase the islands in 1867 and 1902, but both treaties failed to be ratified. The outbreak of World War I in 1914 changed the calculus entirely. The United States grew deeply paranoid about German naval expansion. 

There was a profound fear in Washington D.C. that Germany—which had significant commercial interests in St. Thomas—might invade neutral Denmark and subsequently seize the Danish West Indies. If Germany established a submarine (U-boat) base in the deep natural harbor of Charlotte Amalie, they could effectively blockade the newly opened Panama Canal and control the Caribbean.

## The $25 Million Transaction

Driven by this strategic panic, U.S. Secretary of State Robert Lansing issued what was essentially an ultimatum to Denmark in 1916: sell the islands to the U.S., or risk the U.S. seizing them by military force to prevent them from falling into German hands.

Denmark, eager to shed the economic liability and wishing to remain neutral in the war, agreed. A national plebiscite was held in Denmark in 1916 (where Danish citizens overwhelmingly voted in favor of the sale). Notably, the people living in the Danish West Indies were **not** permitted to vote on their own transfer.

The treaty was signed, and the islands were sold for $25 million in gold coin (approximately $600 million today, adjusted for inflation).

## The Legacy of the Transfer

The Transfer Day of 1917 marked the end of Denmark's transatlantic empire, but it did not bring immediate democracy to the islanders. The U.S. placed the islands under the administration of the Department of the Navy for decades, and the residents would not gain U.S. citizenship until 1927. 

Today, the archives in Copenhagen stand as the primary record of the islands' pre-1917 history—a massive colonial dataset detailing the lives of hundreds of thousands of people whose home was traded between two empires for gold.
