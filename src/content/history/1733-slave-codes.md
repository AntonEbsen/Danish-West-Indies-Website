# The 1733 Slave Codes: State-Sanctioned Terror

To understand the reality of the Danish colonial empire, one must look past the pristine architecture of Copenhagen and examine the laws that funded it. In a colony where enslaved Africans vastly outnumbered their European captors (often by ratios of 10 to 1), the Danish administration could not rely on civic obedience. They had to rely on absolute, state-sanctioned terror.

This terror was legally codified on September 5, 1733, when Governor **Philip Gardelin** issued a horrific set of laws known as the **1733 Slave Codes**.

## The Mathematics of Fear

By the 1730s, the Danish West India and Guinea Company was aggressively expanding its sugar operations, pouring thousands of newly captive Africans into St. Thomas and the newly acquired island of St. Croix. The white population was terrified of an uprising. 

Gardelin’s codes were explicitly designed to break the spirit of the enslaved population through unimaginable cruelty. The laws stripped enslaved people of any semblance of humanity, reducing them legally to property that could be destroyed at will by the state.

## The Mandates of Torture

The 1733 Slave Codes contained 19 specific articles. They did not suggest punishments; they legally mandated them. Governor Gardelin’s chilling edict was published and read aloud to the terrorized population:

> "The leader of runaway negroes shall be pinched three times with red-hot iron, and then hanged." 
> — Article 1, 1733 Slave Code

> "A negro who lifts his hand to strike a white person, or threaten him with violence, shall be pinched and hanged, should the white person demand it, if not, to lose his right hand."
> — Article 8, 1733 Slave Code

> "One negro who steals to the value of 4 rix-dollars, shall be pinched and hanged; less than 4 rix-dollars, to be branded, and receive 150 stripes."
> — Article 10, 1733 Slave Code

Furthermore, the codes legally insulated the planters. It was explicitly declared that an owner could not be punished if an enslaved person died as a result of "lawful" correction.

## The Catalyst for Rebellion

Gardelin's intention was to paralyze the Black population with fear. Instead, the sheer brutality of the 1733 codes had the exact opposite effect: it ignited the very explosion the Danes were trying to prevent.

The enslaved population realized that they had absolutely nothing to lose. Death by rebellion was preferable to the slow, torturous death promised by the Danish legal system.

Just two and a half months after Gardelin published his horrific codes, the enslaved Akwamu people on the neighboring island of St. John rose up in November 1733. They captured the Danish fort, killed the garrison, and took over the entire island in what became one of the longest and most significant slave rebellions in Caribbean history. The rebellion proved that state-sanctioned terror could never extinguish the human drive for freedom.
