# The Schimmelmann Family: Architects of Danish Colonial Wealth

No family embodies the immense wealth—and profound moral contradictions—of Denmark's colonial era quite like the Schimmelmanns. Throughout the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the Schimmelmann dynasty controlled the levers of Danish finance, dictated colonial policy, and operated as the largest slave owners in the Danish West Indies. 

Their vast fortune funded the construction of lavish palaces in Copenhagen, yet it was built entirely upon the brutal labor of enslaved Africans on St. Croix.

## Heinrich Carl von Schimmelmann (1724–1782)

The patriarch of the dynasty, **Heinrich Carl von Schimmelmann**, was a German-born merchant who rose to become the Danish Minister of Finance. He was arguably the wealthiest man in Denmark during his lifetime. 

Schimmelmann recognized that unimaginable wealth could be extracted from the transatlantic triangle trade. In the 1760s, he purchased massive tracts of land on St. Croix and heavily invested in the slave trade on the Gold Coast (Ghana). 

### The Plantations on St. Croix
Schimmelmann owned four massive sugar plantations on St. Croix, the most famous being:
*   **La Princesse**
*   **La Grange**
*   **Hellems**
*   **Caroline's Pleasure**

At their peak, these plantations were worked by over 1,000 enslaved Africans. The labor was grueling, the mortality rates were catastrophic, and the profits were astronomical. Schimmelmann also purchased the sugar refinery in Copenhagen, vertically integrating his empire: he owned the slave ships, the plantations that produced the raw sugar, and the European factories that refined it.

The profits from this blood-soaked enterprise funded the construction of the magnificent **Schimmelmann Mansion** (now the Odd Fellow Palace) in Copenhagen, a stark architectural reminder of where the wealth of the "Florissant" (flourishing) period of Danish history truly originated.

## Ernst Schimmelmann (1747–1831): The Paradox of Abolition

Heinrich’s son, **Ernst Schimmelmann**, inherited both his father's vast colonial empire and his position as Denmark's Minister of Finance. Ernst presents one of the most glaring moral paradoxes of the era.

Influenced by Enlightenment ideals and the growing European abolitionist movement, Ernst Schimmelmann became the political architect behind the **1792 Royal Decree** that made Denmark the first European nation to formally ban the transatlantic slave trade (effective 1803).

### The "Dark Decade" Loophole
However, Ernst was still a massive plantation owner. The 1792 decree was heavily influenced by his own economic interests. He engineered a **10-year transition period** (1792-1802), during which the Danish state provided aggressive state-sponsored loans to planters—including his own family's estates—to purchase as many enslaved people as possible before the ban took effect. 

The goal was to flood the islands with enough young, enslaved women to make the enslaved workforce self-sustaining through natural reproduction. This hypocritical policy resulted in a massive, horrific spike in human trafficking during that final decade.

Furthermore, while Ernst abolished the *trade* of enslaved people across the Atlantic, he furiously defended the institution of *slavery itself* on his St. Croix plantations. He continued to profit immensely from the forced labor of the people his own decree had trapped on the islands.

## Legacy

The Schimmelmann family illustrates the ultimate reality of the Danish colonial project: the absolute intertwining of state power, private capital, and human exploitation. The pristine palaces of Copenhagen and the brutal ruins of the sugar mills on St. Croix are two sides of the same Schimmelmann coin—a legacy that Denmark is only recently beginning to fully confront.
