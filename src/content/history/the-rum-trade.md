# The Rum Trade: Molasses and Distillation

While refined sugar was the primary export of the Danish West Indies, no account of the colonial economy is complete without examining its most lucrative, highly addictive byproduct: **Rum**.

The production of sugar generated massive quantities of molasses and "skimmings" (the impurities boiled off the cane juice). Rather than discarding this waste, planters fermented and distilled it, creating a powerful economic engine that drove the transatlantic slave trade.

## The Chemistry of Empire

The distillation process on a St. Croix estate was a dangerous, highly industrial operation run entirely by enslaved laborers.

1. **Fermentation:** The dark, thick molasses and cane skimmings were mixed with water and left to ferment in massive wooden vats in the sweltering Caribbean heat.
2. **Distillation:** The fermented "wash" was then transferred to copper pot stills. Enslaved workers maintained roaring fires beneath the stills, a brutal task in the already oppressive climate.
3. **Condensation:** The alcohol vapors rose through the "swan neck" of the still and passed through a coiled copper pipe submerged in a tank of cold water, condensing into high-proof, harsh rum known colloquially as "Kill-Devil."

The resulting rum was then aged in oak barrels, which were assembled on the plantation by highly skilled enslaved coopers.

## The Currency of the Triangular Trade

Rum was not just a luxury good exported to the taverns of Copenhagen or the American colonies; it was a foundational currency of the transatlantic slave trade.

The Danish West India and Guinea Company (and later private merchants) operated a brutal economic triangle:
1. **From Denmark to West Africa:** Ships left Copenhagen carrying textiles, firearms, and brass basins to forts like Osu Castle (Christiansborg) in present-day Ghana. 
2. **From West Africa to the Caribbean:** These goods were exchanged with African kingdoms for enslaved human beings, who were then forced across the horrific Middle Passage to the Danish West Indies.
3. **From the Caribbean to West Africa:** Crucially, ships leaving St. Croix did not just return to Europe. Many sailed directly back to the Gold Coast laden with **Cruzian rum**. The rum was highly sought after by African merchants and European fort commanders alike. It was traded directly for *more* enslaved people.

In this grim economic cycle, the labor of enslaved people in the Caribbean directly funded the capture and purchase of more enslaved people in Africa. 

## The Quality of St. Croix Rum

Despite the violent origins of its production, rum from St. Croix (Cruzian rum) gained a massive reputation for its quality. By the late 18th century, St. Croix was dotted with over a hundred distilleries. 

The economic ledgers of the era reveal that for many planters, the sale of rum alone was enough to cover the entire operating costs of the estate, including the purchasing of enslaved labor. The profits from the crystallized sugar were therefore pure, unadulterated profit, shipped back to the merchants of Denmark.

## A Lasting Legacy

The legacy of the Danish rum trade is profound. The wealth generated by this liquid capital enriched the Danish state and prominent families like the Schimmelmanns. Today, rum remains deeply embedded in the culture and economy of the US Virgin Islands, a complex legacy of an industry built upon the unimaginable suffering of enslaved African distillers, coopers, and cane cutters.
